Dimension / grader definitions
  • search_behavior — Code (pass/fail): actual searches vs expected_searches (0 → must not search; N → ≥ N).
  • accuracy — Code (pass/fail): do all of the case's gold_facts appear in the answer? (cheap keyword check, no LLM).
  • citations — Code (pass/fail): when search was used, ≥ 1 citation present and every cited URL resolves (HTTP 200).
  • correctness — Opus judge (0–2): answer vs reference_answer — Correct / Partial / Incorrect.
  • faithfulness — Opus judge (0–2): every claim supported by retrieved text — Grounded / Minor gap / Unsupported.
  • behavior — Opus judge (0–2): special-case handling vs expected_behavior — Ideal / Acceptable / Poor.
Case category definitions
  • single_hop_factual — Direct factual question answerable from one article's summary.
  • multi_hop_factual — Requires chaining several searches to combine facts.
  • deep_section_factual — Detail lives in a body section, so the agent must fetch_section after searching.
  • ambiguous_entity — Entity with several common senses; answer one fully and mention the others.
  • false_premise — Question embeds a wrong assumption; correct it, grounded.
  • non_factual — Arithmetic / translation / reasoning; answer directly, label as not-from-Wikipedia.
  • unanswerable — Private or unknowable; abstain honestly without fabricating.
  • found_but_insufficient — Wikipedia covers the topic but lacks the precise fact; answer the part it has and flag the gap.
  • temporal_recent — Very recent / post-training-cutoff event; ground in Wikipedia or abstain — never answer from memory.
  • temporal_past — A fixed historical fact that cannot change; retrieve and cite.

"Mercury" is one of the most versatile words in the English language! It most commonly refers to **three very different things**: a planet, a chemical element, and a Roman god. Here's a rundown of each: --- ## 🪐 1. Mercury (The Planet) Mercury is the **smallest planet in the Solar System and the closest to the Sun**. Key facts: - **Size:** Diameter of about 4,880 km (3,030 mi) — smaller than some moons. - **Orbit:** One Mercurian year is only **88 Earth days**, but one solar day (sunrise to sunrise) lasts about **176 Earth days**, due to a unique 3:2 spin-orbit resonance. - **Surface:** Heavily cratered, resembling Earth's Moon. Its largest crater, **Caloris Planitia**, is ~1,550 km across. - **Temperature extremes:** Ranges from −170 °C (−270 °F) at night to 420 °C (790 °F) in sunlight. - **Atmosphere:** Virtually none — just a very thin exosphere. - **Magnetic field:** Has a weak magnetic field, just strong enough to deflect some solar wind. - **Water ice:** Its poles are permanently shadowed, strongly suggesting water ice may exist in craters. - **Exploration:** First visited by **Mariner 10** in 1974, later by the **MESSENGER** and **BepiColombo** orbiters. - **Fate:** Expected to be destroyed when the Sun becomes a red giant in ~7–8 billion years. --- ## ⚗️ 2. Mercury (The Element) Mercury (symbol **Hg**, atomic number **80**) is a **chemical element** commonly known as **quicksilver**. Key facts: - It is the **only metallic element that is liquid at standard temperature and pressure**. - It is a heavy, silvery d-block metal with the **lowest freezing and boiling points** of any metal. - It is most commonly found in nature as **cinnabar** (mercuric sulfide), which also yields the red pigment **vermilion**. - **Toxicity:** Mercury is highly toxic to the nervous system, immune system, and kidneys. Mercury poisoning can result from exposure to its water-soluble compounds (e.g., mercuric chloride, methylmercury). - **Uses:** Historically used in thermometers, barometers, fluorescent lamps, dental amalgam, and switches. Its use has declined sharply due to health concerns. --- ## ⚡ 3. Mercury (The Roman God) In Roman religion and mythology, Mercury (**Mercurius**) is a **major deity** and one of the 12 Dii Consentes (the principal Roman gods). Key facts: - He is the god of **commerce, communication, travelers, thieves, luck, eloquence, and financial gain**, and also the **guide of souls to the underworld** (psychopomp). - He was the **son of Jupiter and Maia** (daughter of the Titan Atlas). - He is the **Roman equivalent of the Greek god Hermes**, and shares many characteristics with him. - He is typically depicted holding a **caduceus** (a staff with intertwined snakes), originally a gift from Apollo. - He was an early god linked to the Etruscan deity **Turms**. --- ## Other Notable Uses The name "Mercury" also applies to many other things, including: - **Freddie Mercury**, legendary lead singer of the band Queen - **Mercury Records**, a major music label - **Project Mercury**, NASA's first human spaceflight program - Various newspapers, companies, and more --- Sources: - Mercury (planet) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_(planet) - Mercury (element) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_(element) - Mercury (mythology) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_(mythology) - Mercury (disambiguation) — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury

Q [non_factual]: If all roses are flowers and some flowers fade quickly, can we conclude that all roses fade quickly?
## No, we cannot conclude that all roses fade quickly. This is a classic **logical reasoning** question (not a factual lookup), so I can answer it directly — no Wikipedia search needed. --- ### Breaking Down the Argument The argument has the structure of a **syllogism**: 1. **Premise 1:** All roses are flowers. *(All R are F)* 2. **Premise 2:** Some flowers fade quickly. *(Some F are Q)* 3. **Proposed Conclusion:** All roses fade quickly. *(All R are Q)* --- ### Why the Conclusion is Invalid This is a **logical fallacy** — specifically, an **undistributed middle** / **illicit particular**. Here's why: - Premise 2 only tells us that **some** flowers fade quickly — not **which** ones. - The roses could be among the flowers that **don't** fade quickly. - Just because roses belong to the set of flowers, and *some* flowers have a property, does **not** mean roses have that property. ### A Simple Illustration Imagine flowers = {roses, tulips, daisies}, and only tulips and daisies fade quickly. Then: - ✅ All roses are flowers. *(true)* - ✅ Some flowers (tulips, daisies) fade quickly. *(true)* - ❌ All roses fade quickly. *(false — roses don't fade quickly in this scenario)* --- ### What *Could* We Validly Conclude? At most, we could say: **"Some flowers that are roses *might* fade quickly"** — but even that is not guaranteed. We simply **cannot draw any certain conclusion** about roses fading quickly from these two premises alone. > **Bottom line:** The argument is **logically invalid**. The conclusion does not follow from the premises.
